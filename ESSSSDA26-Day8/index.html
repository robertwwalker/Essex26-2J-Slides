Motivation and Taxonomy

Why Standard Panel Estimators Fail

The linear panel model eliminates \mu_i via the Within transformation:

\tilde{y}_{it} = \tilde{x}'_{it}\beta + \tilde{v}_{it}

This works because demeaning is a linear operation — \mu_i cancels exactly.

The nonlinear panel model with binary, count, censored, or ordered y_{it}:

\Pr[y_{it} = 1 \mid x_{it}, \mu_i] = F(x'_{it}\beta + \mu_i)

F(\cdot) is nonlinear \Rightarrow \mu_i cannot be swept out algebraically.

Three consequences:

  • MLE of \beta is inconsistent (incidental parameters problem)
  • Partial effects \partial F / \partial x_{it} are contaminated by \hat{\mu}_i
  • Standard panel “fixes” (first-differences, Within) are no longer valid

The key insight is that F(a + b) \neq F(a) + F(b) in general, so applying the Within transformation to the arguments of F(\cdot) does not eliminate \mu_i. This is the fundamental obstacle. For the linear model, the information matrix is block-diagonal between \beta and the \mu_i parameters (Hsiao 2003), so MLE of \beta and \{\mu_i\} are asymptotically independent. For nonlinear models this block-diagonality breaks down, and the inconsistency in \hat{\mu}_i (estimated from only T observations each) propagates into \hat{\beta}.

A Unified Latent Variable Framework

A latent continuous y^*_{it} underlies all limited dependent variable models:

y^*_{it} = x'_{it}\beta + \mu_i + v_{it}

Different observability rules generate different model families:

Outcome Type Observability Rule Example
Binary choice y_{it} = \mathbf{1}[y^*_{it} > 0] Employment
Ordered y_{it} = j if \kappa_{j-1} < y^*_{it} \leq \kappa_j Terror scale
Censored (Tobit) y_{it} = \max(0,\, y^*_{it}) Hours worked
Truncated y_{it} = y^*_{it} if y^*_{it} > 0 Positive wages
Count \lambda_{it} = \exp(x'_{it}\beta + \mu_i) Patents, arrests

For count models the latent variable interpretation is slightly different: we work with E[y_{it} \mid \mu_i, x_{it}] = \exp(x'_{it}\beta + \mu_i), where \mu_i acts as a multiplicative scaling factor on the expected count. For the binary/ordered/censored cases, v_{it} is typically assumed normal (probit/Tobit) or logistic (logit), symmetric around zero, so that \Pr[y_{it} = 1] = F(x'_{it}\beta + \mu_i).

Roadmap: A Three-Axis Taxonomy

Three dimensions determine which estimator to use:

Axis 1 — Outcome type: Binary \to Ordered \to Count \to Censored / Truncated

Axis 2 — Effect treatment: Fixed effects (FE) \to Random effects (RE) \to Correlated RE (Mundlak/Chamberlain)

Axis 3 — Temporal structure: Static \to Observation-driven Markov \to Parameter-driven state space

After heterogeneity, we will have a dynamics taxonomy; at the end, there is a complete decision flowchart.

This three-axis taxonomy organises the entire deck [and the class]. The FE vs RE choice depends on whether \mu_i is correlated with x_{it} (Hausman test). The temporal structure choice depends on whether past outcomes y_{i,t-1} directly enter the conditional distribution (observation-driven) or dynamics arise through a latent evolving process (parameter-driven). The observation-driven approach has closed-form likelihoods; the parameter-driven approach requires integration over the latent path.

The Incidental Parameters Problem

The Problem Stated Precisely

Setting: N individuals, T periods, individual effects \{\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_N\} unknown.

As N \to \infty with T fixed, the number of nuisance parameters grows at the same rate as the sample — the MLE for (\beta, \mu_1,\ldots,\mu_N) is inconsistent for \beta.

Why it does not arise in the linear model:

The Within estimator eliminates \mu_i exactly before estimation. The MLE of \beta and \{\mu_i\} are asymptotically independent in the linear case (Hsiao 2003).

Why it arises in nonlinear models:

Each \hat{\mu}_i is estimated from only T observations. Inconsistency in \hat{\mu}_i is transmitted to \hat{\beta} because the nonlinear information matrix is not block-diagonal between \beta and \{\mu_i\}.

Neyman and Scott (1948) first identified this problem. The fundamental issue is that with T fixed, each \mu_i is estimated imprecisely, and this imprecision feeds into the estimate of \beta through the cross-derivatives in the Hessian of the log-likelihood. For the logit model specifically, Chamberlain (1980) and Hsiao (2003) demonstrate the bias analytically.

Magnitude of the Bias: Monte Carlo Evidence

For fixed effects logit as N \to \infty with T fixed:

T Asymptotic bias in \hat{\beta}_{MLE} Direction
2 \hat{\beta}_{MLE} \xrightarrow{p} 2\beta Away from zero
10 \approx 16\% Away from zero
20 \approx 6.9\% Away from zero

Sources: Hsiao (2003); Greene (2004a)

  • Bias is always away from zero (upward in absolute value)
  • At N = 1000, T = 2: bias \approx 100\% (Greene 2004a)
  • For probit the bias is similar in direction but slightly smaller
  • Exception: Fixed effects Poisson has no incidental parameters problem.

Greene (2004a) runs Monte Carlo with N = 1000 and T = 2, 3, 5, 8, 10, 20 and 200 replications, finding these bias percentages. The direction is always away from zero: the MLE overestimates |\beta|. For probit, Heckman (1981b) found at most 10% bias in early experiments; Greene (2004a) finds 10-24% bias always away from zero. The Poisson exception is the most important: the conditioning argument works perfectly for Poisson, giving no incidental parameters problem at all.

Three Strategies for the Incidental Parameters Problem

Strategy 1 — Condition out \mu_i (FE approach)

Find a sufficient statistic S_i for \mu_i; condition the likelihood on S_i. \mu_i is eliminated without being estimated. \to Exact for logit (Chamberlain 1980) and Poisson (Hausman et al. 1984). \to Not available for probit or Tobit in general.

Strategy 2 — Integrate out \mu_i (RE approach)

Assume \mu_i \sim F(\sigma_\mu^2); replace \mu_i by integrating over its distribution. \to Consistent if distribution correctly specified; correlated RE (Mundlak) relaxes independence.

Strategy 3 — Model and correct the bias (Modified MLE)

Reduce bias from O(1/T) to O(1/T^2) via jackknife or analytic correction. \to Carro (2007); Hahn & Newey (2004); especially useful for dynamic models.

Strategy 1 is exact and requires no distributional assumptions on \mu_i, but requires the logistic distribution (for binary) or Poisson distribution (for counts). Strategy 2 is broadly applicable but requires correct specification of the distribution of \mu_i. The Mundlak (1978) device (\mu_i = \bar{x}'_i\delta + \varepsilon_i) relaxes the independence assumption. Strategy 3 is valuable for dynamic models where strategies 1 and 2 face additional complications from the initial conditions problem.

The Incidental Parameters Problem in Dynamic Models

In static models the IPP involves only \beta and \{\mu_i\}.

In dynamic models with y_{i,t-1} as a regressor:

\Pr[y_{it} = 1 \mid x_{it}, y_{i,t-1}, \mu_i] = F(x'_{it}\beta + \gamma y_{i,t-1} + \mu_i)

Why it is worse dynamically:

  • y_{i,t-1} is itself a function of \mu_i (generated by the same model)
  • This introduces a second bias channel in \hat{\gamma}, beyond the standard IPP
  • The initial condition y_{i0} is correlated with \mu_i by construction

The initial conditions problem: y_{i0} is typically not exogenous. Ignoring this biases estimates of \gamma (state dependence) and compounds the IPP bias. \to We will address dynamic complications systematically after heterogeneity.

Heckman (1981a,b,c) first systematically studied dynamic limited dependent variable panel models and identified the initial conditions problem. The key issue is that if we start observing the panel at t = 1, the initial value y_{i0} was generated by the model at some earlier time, making it correlated with \mu_i. Treating y_{i0} as exogenous introduces bias in \hat{\gamma}. Heckman (1981b) proposed a reduced-form approximation; Wooldridge (2005) proposed a simpler conditional approach that dominates in practice.

Fixed Effects Binary Choice — Conditional MLE

Chamberlain’s Sufficient Statistic

Key result (Chamberlain 1980): For the fixed effects logit, S_i = \sum_{t=1}^T y_{it} is a minimal sufficient statistic for \mu_i.

Conditional on S_i, the joint distribution of (y_{i1},\ldots,y_{iT}) does not depend on \mu_i.

L_c(\beta) = \prod_{i=1}^N \Pr\!\left(y_{i1},\ldots,y_{iT} \;\Big|\; \sum_{t=1}^T y_{it}\right)

Maximising L_c gives the CMLE — consistent and asymptotically normal as N \to \infty for fixed T. No incidental parameters problem.

The sufficient statistic result follows from the exponential family structure of the logistic likelihood. Conditioning on \sum_t y_{it} cancels \mu_i from both numerator and denominator of the conditional probability, leaving a likelihood depending only on \beta. This is special to the logistic distribution — it does not hold for the normal (probit), which is why there is no analogous FE probit.

The T = 2 Closed Form

For T = 2, conditioning on y_{i1} + y_{i2} = 1 (only “switchers” contribute):

\Pr[y_{i1}=1, y_{i2}=0 \mid y_{i1}+y_{i2}=1] = \frac{1}{1 + e^{(x_{i2}-x_{i1})'\beta}}

\Pr[y_{i1}=0, y_{i2}=1 \mid y_{i1}+y_{i2}=1] = \frac{1}{1 + e^{(x_{i1}-x_{i2})'\beta}}

Key observations:

  • \mu_i has completely dropped out — consistent estimation possible
  • Units with y_{i1} = y_{i2} (non-switchers) contribute nothing to L_c
  • Equivalent to standard logit on the “switcher” subsample with \Delta x_i as regressors

Equations (11.10) and (11.11) in Baltagi. The T=2 CMLE can be implemented as a standard logit of \mathbf{1}[\Delta y_i = 1] on \Delta x_i = x_{i2} - x_{i1}, restricted to the switcher subsample. In Stata: clogit y x1 x2, group(id) or xtlogit y x1 x2, fe. An important practical concern: non-switchers are discarded, which can dramatically reduce the effective sample size in many social science panels where binary outcomes are stable (e.g., employment, treaty ratification).

Generalising to T > 2 and Manski’s Alternative

For T > 2: \sum_t y_{it} can be 0,1,\ldots,T. Observations with \sum_t y_{it} \in \{0,T\} contribute nothing. Conditional probabilities computed recursively — implemented in xtlogit, fe and clogit.

Manski’s (1987) maximum score estimator: Semiparametric alternative when the logistic assumption is too strong:

\max_\beta \; \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n z_i \cdot \text{sgn}(x'_i \beta), \quad z_i = 2y_i - 1

Maximises correct predictions; no likelihood assumed.

Limitations: Convergence rate N^{1/3} not \sqrt{N}; no information-matrix standard errors — bootstrap required.

Problem 11.1 in Baltagi asks the student to extend the T=2 case to T=3. For T=3, conditioning is on \sum_t y_{it} \in \{1,2\}. The conditional probabilities involve ratios of sums of exponentials and can be computed analytically but are more complex than the T=2 case.

Manski’s maximum score estimator is consistent under conditions weaker than the logit model — it allows the distribution of v_{it} to be unknown. The N^{1/3} convergence rate is a significant disadvantage for inference in practical samples.

Testing: Hausman Test for Individual Effects

H_0: No individual effects — pooled logit is consistent and efficient.

Under H_1: CMLE is consistent; pooled MLE is not.

m = (\hat{\beta}_{CML} - \hat{\beta}_{MLE})' \left[\widehat{V}_{CML} - \widehat{V}_{MLE}\right]^{-1} (\hat{\beta}_{CML} - \hat{\beta}_{MLE}) \;\sim\; \chi^2_K

under H_0.

Practical note: CMLE discards non-switchers so it is less efficient under H_0. The test asks: is the efficiency cost worth the bias protection under H_1? An alternative: use the Mundlak test in xtprobit

The constant must be dropped from the pooled logit for this comparison since CMLE has no constant. The variance difference \widehat{V}_{CML} - \widehat{V}_{MLE} should be PSD under H_0; if not, use a generalised inverse. Many applied researchers skip the Hausman test and justify the FE approach on substantive grounds (correlation between \mu_i and x_{it} is likely).

Semiparametric Extensions and Bounds

Honoré & Lewbel (2002): Chamberlain’s negative identification result can be overturned if at least one continuous “special regressor” is independent of \mu_i. Allows \sqrt{N}-consistent semiparametric estimation without the logistic assumption.

Magnac (2004): \sum_t y_{it} is a sufficient statistic under necessary and sufficient conditions strictly weaker than the logit model — the result generalises beyond logit to the “odds ratio” restriction.

Honoré & Tamer (2006): When point identification fails, bounds on \beta can be constructed. The identified set is often informatively small in practice — useful when exact point identification requires implausible assumptions.

Honoré and Lewbel (2002) identify the key requirement for semiparametric root-N consistency: a single continuous regressor whose coefficient is known and whose variation is independent of \mu_i and v_{it}. This “special regressor” condition is restrictive but sometimes naturally available (e.g., exogenous price variation).

Honoré and Tamer (2006) use moment inequalities to construct the identified set. Their bounds can be computed using linear programming and are often quite informative. This is increasingly important in political science and economics where the logistic distribution assumption is routinely questioned.

Random Effects Binary Choice

The T-Dimensional Integral Problem

For RE probit, u_{it} = \mu_i + v_{it}, where \mu_i \sim \text{IIN}(0,\sigma_\mu^2), v_{it} \sim \text{IIN}(0,1).

The individual likelihood is a T-dimensional integral — infeasible for large T.

Conditioning on \mu_i makes the v_{it} independent, reducing to a single integral:

L_i = \int \left[\prod_{t=1}^T \Phi\!\left(q_{it}(x'_{it}\beta + \mu_i)\right)\right] \phi\!\left(\frac{\mu_i}{\sigma_\mu}\right) \frac{d\mu_i}{\sigma_\mu}

where q_{it} = 2y_{it} - 1 and \Phi is the standard normal CDF.

Equations (11.12) and (11.13) in Baltagi. The key step: write the joint density as the product of conditional densities of v_{it} given \mu_i, times the marginal density of \mu_i. Integrating over v_{it} is analytically available for probit (giving \Phi(\cdot)), leaving only the integral over \mu_i. For the logit model, the integral over v_{it} doesn’t simplify when \mu_i is normally distributed — this is one reason RE probit is more commonly used than RE logit with normal random effects.

Butler–Moffitt (1982) Gauss–Hermite Quadrature

The single remaining integral is approximated by Gauss–Hermite quadrature:

\ln L_h = \sum_{i=1}^N \ln\!\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}} \sum_{h=1}^H w_h \prod_{t=1}^T \Phi\!\left(q_{it}(x'_{it}\beta + \theta z_h)\right)\right]

where \theta = \sigma_\mu\sqrt{2}, and w_h, z_h are quadrature weights and nodes.

  • H = number of quadrature points; accuracy increases with H
  • Default H = 12 in Stata; H = 24 recommended for larger \sigma_\mu
  • Adaptive Gauss–Hermite quadrature (AGQ): More accurate, default in lme4 (R)

The Butler-Moffitt approximation uses \int g(x) e^{-x^2} dx \approx \sum_{h=1}^H w_h g(z_h) where w_h and z_h are Gauss-Hermite weights and nodes. With the change of variables \mu_i = \theta z, the integral over the normal density becomes a Gaussian integral. The accuracy depends on H and on \sigma_\mu: when \sigma_\mu is large, more quadrature points are needed. In Stata, use intpoints(#) to increase H. In R, lme4::glmer() has the nAGQ argument; AGQ (adaptive quadrature) centres the quadrature at the conditional mode of \mu_i given the data, substantially improving accuracy.

Mundlak Device and Chamberlain Correlated RE

Problem with basic RE probit: Assumes \mu_i \perp x_{it} — often unrealistic.

Mundlak (1978) device: Let \mu_i = \bar{x}'_i \delta + \varepsilon_i where \bar{x}_i = T^{-1}\sum_t x_{it}.

\Pr[y_{it} = 1 \mid x_{it}, \bar{x}_i] = \Phi\!\left((1+\sigma_\varepsilon^2)^{-1/2}(x'_{it}\beta + \bar{x}'_i \delta)\right)

Consistent even when \mu_i is correlated with x_{it}. Implement by adding \bar{x}_i as regressors to xtprobit.

Chamberlain (1984) extension: \mu_i = x'_{i1}a_1 + \cdots + x'_{iT}a_T + \varepsilon_i — more flexible but uses TK additional parameters.

Test: H_0: \delta = 0 in Mundlak specification tests whether RE assumption is valid.

The Mundlak (1978) device is a pragmatic compromise between FE and RE. By including \bar{x}_i as additional regressors, the model absorbs the correlation between \mu_i and x_{it} through the projection E[\mu_i | x_i] = \bar{x}'_i\delta. The test of H_0: \delta = 0 using a Wald test after xtprobit is an alternative to the Hausman test that is more robust to heteroskedasticity and can be computed directly in standard RE software.

The Chamberlain (1984) extension is equivalent to the Mundlak device when T is moderate, but uses more degrees of freedom. For large K (many regressors) and small T, the Mundlak device is preferred. For small K and moderate T, the Chamberlain specification may be worth the additional parameters.

Choosing Between FE Logit and RE Probit

FE Conditional Logit

  • No distributional assumption on \mu_i
  • Allows arbitrary \text{Corr}(\mu_i, x_{it})
  • Requires logistic distribution for v_{it}
  • Discards all non-switchers
  • No partial effects for time-invariant x

RE Probit (Mundlak)

  • Uses full sample
  • Normal distribution for v_{it}
  • Partial effects for time-invariant x available
  • Requires distributional assumption on \mu_i
  • Consistent even if \text{Corr}(\mu_i, x_{it}) \neq 0 (via Mundlak)

Practical recommendation: Use correlated RE probit (Mundlak device) as the default. Report FE logit as a robustness check. Test H_0: \delta = 0 to guide the choice.

In most social science applications with not-too-short panels (T \geq 5), the Mundlak-device RE probit is a good default: it allows correlation between \mu_i and x_{it}, uses the full sample, and allows partial effects for time-invariant variables. The FE conditional logit is preferred when: (1) the logistic distribution is credible, (2) the non-switcher loss is modest, and (3) strict robustness to the distribution of \mu_i is required.

Ordinal Outcomes — Static Models

The Ordered Latent Variable Model

Observable y_{it} \in \{1,2,\ldots,J\} generated by threshold-crossing of y^*_{it}:

y_{it} = j \iff \kappa_{j-1} < y^*_{it} \leq \kappa_j, \quad -\infty = \kappa_0 < \kappa_1 < \cdots < \kappa_J = +\infty

With panel structure: y^*_{it} = x'_{it}\beta + \mu_i + v_{it}

\Pr[y_{it} = j \mid x_{it}, \mu_i] = F(\kappa_j - x'_{it}\beta - \mu_i) - F(\kappa_{j-1} - x'_{it}\beta - \mu_i)

The panel complication: \mu_i shifts the entire probability distribution — the same incidental parameters problem as in binary choice, but across J categories.

No sufficient statistic for \mu_i in general for J > 2 — hence no FE ordered probit analogous to FE logit.

The ordered probit/logit assumes proportional odds: the same \beta governs all thresholds. For political science: the Political Terror Scale (PTS) runs 1–5, where 1 = secure rule of law and 5 = terror extending to the whole population. With country fixed effects, \mu_i captures time-invariant country characteristics (historical institutions, colonial legacy) that shift the entire repression level. The \kappa_j thresholds are estimated along with \beta. The absence of a sufficient statistic for \mu_i (unlike the binary logit case) is why FE ordered logit requires the BUC workaround discussed next.

Random Effects Ordered Probit

Direct extension of RE Probit: \mu_i \sim \text{IIN}(0,\sigma_\mu^2) independent of v_{it} \sim \text{IIN}(0,1).

The individual likelihood involves the same single integral as RE probit — Butler–Moffitt quadrature applies directly.

Stata: xtoprobit y x1 x2, intpoints(24)

R: ordinal::clmm(ordered(y) ~ x1 + x2 + (1|id), link = "probit")

Correlated RE (Mundlak device): Add \bar{x}_i as regressors to allow \text{Corr}(\mu_i, x_{it}) \neq 0.

Post-estimation: margins / marginaleffects for category-specific average partial effects.

RE ordered probit is the workhorse for ordered panel outcomes. In Stata, xtoprobit uses adaptive Gauss-Hermite quadrature by default. In R, ordinal::clmm() provides flexible options including different link functions (probit, logistic, complementary log-log) and random effects structures. The marginaleffects package in R provides avg_slopes() for average partial effects on each probability \Pr[y_{it} = j], which vary by category — the partial effect on \Pr[y_{it} = 1] typically has the opposite sign from the partial effect on \Pr[y_{it} = J].

Fixed Effects Ordered Logit — The BUC Estimator

Problem: No general sufficient statistic for \mu_i in the ordered logit with J > 2.

Baetschmann, Staub & Winkelmann (2015) — “Blow-Up and Cluster” (BUC):

  1. For each threshold j \in \{1,\ldots,J-1\}, dichotomise: \tilde{y}_{it}^{(j)} = \mathbf{1}[y_{it} > j]
  2. Run FE conditional logit on each binary outcome \tilde{y}_{it}^{(j)} separately
  3. “Blow-up”: Stack all J-1 binary datasets into one long dataset
  4. “Cluster”: Constrain \beta equal across j; cluster-robust SEs for dependence

Stata: ssc install buc then buc y x1 x2, group(id) vce(cluster id)

R: bife::bife() for each binary collapse, then stack and cluster

Baetschmann, Staub and Winkelmann (2015) prove the BUC estimator is consistent and asymptotically normal under fixed T as N \to \infty, provided the true model is an ordered logit with proportional odds. The key insight: any binary collapse of an ordered logit is also a logit with the same \beta but different thresholds. Conditioning on the binary sum within each collapsed dataset eliminates individual effects. Pooling across all binary collapses uses all available information. The cluster-robust SEs account for the fact that the same individual appears in J-1 binary datasets.

An alternative is Ferrer-i-Carbonell and Frijters (2004), which uses a different computational strategy but similar idea. For very long panels (T \to \infty), Carro and Traferri (2014) derive an analytic bias correction for FE ordered probit MLE.

Generalised Ordered Models and Parallel Regression

The parallel regression assumption: Same \beta governs all thresholds j. Often violated.

Generalised ordered logit: Allow \beta to vary across thresholds:

\Pr[y_{it} > j \mid x_{it}] = F(x'_{it}\beta_j), \quad j = 1,\ldots,J-1

Stata: gologit2 (Williams 2006) — partial proportionality available (pl() option)

R: VGAM::vglm(y ~ x, family = cumulative(parallel = FALSE))

Testing: Brant test in Stata (brant after ologit); LR test comparing constrained vs unconstrained models.

Panel extension: xtgologit2 (community SSC) for RE generalised ordered probit; Mundlak device as workaround for FE.

Williams (2006) proposes gologit2 as a flexible implementation allowing partial proportionality: some coefficients are constrained to be equal across thresholds while others are allowed to vary. This is useful when the proportional odds assumption fails for some but not all regressors.

Das and van Soest (1999) find that the proportional odds assumption is rejected for subjective income expectations, motivating the generalised ordered model. Their dynamic extension naturally accommodates the generalised ordered structure with lagged category-specific effects.

For the PTS application: it is worth testing proportional odds since different covariates (e.g., democracy, GDP) may have qualitatively different effects at low vs high repression levels. This is common advice, and very bad advice.

Panel Count Models — Static

Why Counts Need Special Treatment

Panel count outcomes: patents, doctor visits, arrests, protest events, conflict incidents.

The natural model — Poisson with E[y_{it} \mid x_{it}, \mu_i] = \exp(x'_{it}\beta + \mu_i) — faces three complications:

  1. Incidental parameters: \mu_i cannot be swept out by demeaning \exp(\cdot)
  2. Overdispersion: \text{Var}[y_{it}] > E[y_{it}] is ubiquitous in practice
  3. Excess zeros: Many panels have a large fraction of zero counts

But: The FE Poisson model has a unique property that exempts it from the incidental parameters problem — the most important positive result in this chapter.

Count data are pervasive in political science (conflict events, legislative bills, coup attempts), economics (patents, strikes, job changes), sociology (arrests, divorces), and public health (hospital visits, disease incidence). The multiplicative form \exp(x'_{it}\beta + \mu_i) = \exp(x'_{it}\beta) \cdot \exp(\mu_i) means that \mu_i acts as a multiplicative scaling factor on the expected count — the natural generalisation of the additive \mu_i in the linear model. This multiplicative structure generates a likelihood in the exponential family with sufficient statistic \sum_t y_{it}, which is the key to the FE Poisson result.

Fixed Effects Poisson — The “Blessing”

Hausman, Hall & Griliches (1984): S_i = \sum_{t=1}^T y_{it} is a sufficient statistic for \mu_i in the Poisson model — and, uniquely, there is no incidental parameters problem.

The conditional likelihood reduces to a multinomial likelihood depending only on \beta:

\Pr\!\left[y_{it} \;\Big|\; \sum_t y_{it}\right] \;\propto\; \prod_t \left(\frac{\exp(x'_{it}\beta)}{\sum_s \exp(x'_{is}\beta)}\right)^{y_{it}}

Wooldridge (1999) QMLE: FE Poisson is consistent even if the count distribution is misspecified, provided E[y_{it} \mid \mu_i, x_{it}] = \exp(x'_{it}\beta + \mu_i) holds. Only the conditional mean needs to be correctly specified.

Stata: xtpoisson y x1 x2, fe vce(cluster id)

R: fixest::fepois(y ~ x1 + x2 | id, vcov = ~ id, data = df)

The HHG (1984) result is remarkable: unlike the binary logit case (where CMLE discards non-switchers), the FE Poisson CMLE uses all observations and has no incidental parameters bias. All individuals contribute to the conditional likelihood, including those with constant count patterns.

Wooldridge’s (1999) QMLE result strengthens the FE Poisson even further: it is consistent under misspecification of the count distribution (overdispersion, zero-inflation) as long as the conditional mean is correctly specified. The robust sandwich variance estimator (vce(cluster id) in Stata) provides valid standard errors under any pattern of overdispersion and serial correlation.

This makes FE Poisson the recommended default for panel count data in most social science applications.

Overdispersion and the Negative Binomial

Overdispersion (\text{Var}[y_{it}] > E[y_{it}]) is nearly universal in practice.

Approach 1 — RE Negative Binomial (HHG 1984): Assume \exp(\mu_i) \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha,\alpha). Integrating out \mu_i gives the NB marginal distribution. Stata: xtnbreg, re

Approach 2 — Robust FE Poisson (recommended): Use FE Poisson QMLE with robust standard errors. Overdispersion inflates SE but does not bias \hat{\beta}. Stata: xtpoisson, fe vce(cluster id)

Warning on xtnbreg, fe: The FE NB in Stata absorbs \mu_i through the overdispersion parameter (not the mean) — Allison & Waterman (2002) showed this does NOT fully control for time-invariant heterogeneity. Use with caution; prefer robust FE Poisson.

The Allison-Waterman (2002) critique of xtnbreg, fe is important: Stata’s FE NB parameterisation allows the overdispersion parameter \alpha to vary by individual, absorbing \mu_i there rather than in the mean. This is different from what most researchers assume when they use “FE NB” — it does not eliminate all time-invariant unobserved heterogeneity from the mean equation. The practical consequence is that xtnbreg, fe estimates may be biased in ways that differ from the Poisson FE.

Wooldridge’s (1999) recommendation: use FE Poisson QMLE with robust standard errors as the primary estimator, even when there is overdispersion. The overdispersion affects efficiency but not consistency. Only if there is strong evidence of systematic misspecification of the conditional mean should one consider alternatives.

Dynamics — The Unifying Framework

Cox’s Taxonomy: Two Types of Dynamic Models

Cox (1981) distinguishes two fundamental classes of dynamic models:

Observation-driven: The conditional distribution f(y_{it} \mid \mathcal{F}_{i,t-1}, x_{it}, \mu_i) depends on past outcomes directly through the filtration \mathcal{F}_{i,t-1} = \{y_{i,t-1}, y_{i,t-2}, \ldots\}. \to Likelihood is a product of closed-form one-period-ahead predictive densities. \to Examples: dynamic logit/probit, ordered Markov, INGARCH.

Parameter-driven: A latent process \alpha_{it} evolves by its own Markov law; y_{it} depends on \alpha_{it} and x_{it} but not directly on y_{i,t-1}. \to Likelihood requires integrating over the path of \alpha_{it} — computationally intensive. \to Examples: state space probit, dynamic factor models with discrete outcomes.

Cox (1981) “Statistical Analysis of Time Series: Some Recent Developments” introduced this distinction in the context of continuous-time series, but it applies equally well to discrete-time panel models. The distinction has profound consequences for computation: observation-driven models have tractable closed-form likelihoods (as products of one-period-ahead predictive densities), while parameter-driven models require numerical integration over the path of the latent process.

In the parameter-driven case, the integral over the path of \alpha_{it} is a T-dimensional integral for a panel of length T, which quickly becomes infeasible as T grows. This motivates the simulation estimation methods (GHK simulator).

For the observation-driven case, the key is that y_{i,t-1} enters the conditional distribution directly. This means that the “state” of the system at time t is fully summarised by (y_{i,t-1}, x_{it}, \mu_i) — we do not need to track the entire history \mathcal{F}_{i,t-1}. This is the first-order Markov property.

True vs Spurious State Dependence

Heckman (1981c) distinguishes two reasons for persistence in panel outcomes:

True state dependence: The past outcome y_{i,t-1} causally changes future probabilities.

\Pr[y_{it} = 1 \mid x_{it}, y_{i,t-1}=1, \mu_i] > \Pr[y_{it} = 1 \mid x_{it}, y_{i,t-1}=0, \mu_i]

Spurious state dependence: Persistence driven by unobserved \mu_i that is stable over time — certain individuals always have high (or low) probabilities regardless of history.

Why it matters: True state dependence implies interventions should be early (to break the cycle). Spurious state dependence implies targeting the underlying heterogeneity.

Testing: \gamma \neq 0 in F(x'_{it}\beta + \gamma y_{i,t-1} + \mu_i), after controlling for \mu_i.

Heckman (1981c) used this distinction in the context of unemployment, arguing that unemployment can be “contagious” (true state dependence through discouragement, skill loss, and employer stigma) or merely reflective of stable individual characteristics (spurious state dependence through ability, motivation, or health). The empirical challenge is that both generate positive autocorrelation in observed outcomes.

A critical point: if we observe \hat{\gamma} \neq 0 in a simple dynamic probit that ignores \mu_i, we cannot determine whether this is true or spurious state dependence. Only after properly controlling for individual heterogeneity (through FE conditioning or RE integration) can we interpret \hat{\gamma} as true state dependence.

In the human rights literature, “path dependence” in repression could reflect either true state dependence (repressive institutions self-reinforce) or spurious state dependence (countries with strong authoritarian characteristics always repress). Walker (2006) addresses this using an ordered Markov model.

The Initial Conditions Problem: A Unified Treatment

In any dynamic panel GLM, y_{i0} is typically not exogenous — it was generated by the same model containing \mu_i.

Three solutions, applicable across all outcome types:

Heckman’s (1981b) approach involves specifying a parametric model for the initial conditions distribution f(y_{i0} | x_i, \mu_i) and integrating over \mu_i jointly. This is flexible but requires specifying the distribution of \mu_i given the initial conditions and covariates, which is a non-trivial modelling decision.

Wooldridge’s (2005) approach avoids this by modelling the conditional distribution of \mu_i given observed variables: \mu_i | y_{i0}, x_i \sim \text{Normal}(y_{i0}\gamma_0 + \bar{x}'_i \delta, \sigma_a^2). This is a Mundlak-type correlated RE specification augmented with the initial condition y_{i0}. The model is then estimated as a standard RE model with y_{i0} and \bar{x}_i as additional regressors. Wooldridge shows this approach is consistent under the stated conditions and is generally more robust than Heckman’s approach.

Dynamic Binary Panel Models

The Dynamic Binary Choice Model

The first-order Markov binary panel model:

\Pr[y_{it} = 1 \mid x_{it}, y_{i,t-1}, \mu_i] = F(x'_{it}\beta + \gamma y_{i,t-1} + \mu_i)

\gamma \neq 0 implies true state dependence (conditional on individual effects).

Key challenge: The incidental parameters problem gets worse — both \mu_i and the lagged outcome y_{i,t-1} create bias channels in the MLE.

Testing state dependence: An LR test of H_0: \gamma = 0 after properly controlling for \mu_i is the cleanest diagnostic. A Wald test also works.

Static model bias from ignoring dynamics: If \gamma \neq 0, omitting y_{i,t-1} from the static model causes omitted variable bias in \hat{\beta} (through the correlation of x_{it} with past outcomes).

The dynamic binary choice panel model is the workhorse for studying state dependence in panel settings. It is widely used in labour economics (union membership, employment), political science (war initiation, repression), and health economics (health status, insurance take-up).

The bias from ignoring dynamics is subtle: if the true model includes \gamma y_{i,t-1}, then omitting it introduces omitted variable bias in the static model even if we control for \mu_i — because y_{i,t-1} is correlated with x_{it} (through the autocorrelation of x_{it}) and with \mu_i (because y_{i,t-1} was generated by a model containing \mu_i). The direction of bias in \hat{\beta} depends on the sign of \gamma and the correlation between x_{it} and y_{i,t-1}.

Wooldridge (2005) Initial Conditions Device in Practice

Step-by-step implementation:

The Wooldridge (2005) device is elegant in its simplicity: it requires only one additional step (computing group means and including the initial condition y_{i0}) and can be implemented in any RE probit routine. The key assumption is that \mu_i | y_{i0}, x_i is normally distributed — a parametric assumption that allows correlation between \mu_i and x_i through the projection E[\mu_i | y_{i0}, x_i] = \gamma_0 y_{i0} + \bar{x}'_i \delta.

In R, the Wooldridge device is implemented by: 1. Computing xbar = within-person mean of each covariate 2. Including y0 (initial condition) and xbar1, xbar2, ... as additional regressors in glmer() or pglm() 3. Using the random = ~ 1 | id random effect structure

Wooldridge (2005) shows via simulations that this approach performs well in typical panels (N = 1000, T = 4, 8), even when the distribution of \mu_i | y_{i0}, x_i is not exactly normal.

Honoré & Kyriazidou (2000): Semiparametric FE Dynamic Logit

For the dynamic FE logit model, the sufficient statistic argument does not directly extend because y_{i,t-1} is correlated with \mu_i.

Honoré & Kyriazidou (2000): Identification requires the strict exogeneity condition x'_{i2} = x'_{i3} for T = 3. Under this condition, a conditional likelihood is available that eliminates \mu_i.

Kernel weighting: Since x'_{i2} = x'_{i3} exactly is a zero-probability event with continuous x, kernel weights K\!\left(\frac{x_{i2}-x_{i3}}{h_N}\right) are applied.

Limitation: Convergence rate slower than \sqrt{N}; computationally intensive.

Practical guidance: For most applied datasets, the Wooldridge (2005) device is more practical.

Honoré and Kyriazidou (2000) is the main semiparametric approach to the dynamic FE binary logit model. The key identification insight is that when x_{i2} = x_{i3} (or close to it, handled by kernel weighting), the sufficient statistic argument can be applied to the conditional likelihood — the conditioning on y_{i1} + y_{i2} and y_{i2} + y_{i3} eliminates both \mu_i and the initial condition bias simultaneously.

The requirement x'_{i2} = x'_{i3} is a strong restriction: it requires that the covariates take the same value in two consecutive periods for identification. In practice, this means the estimator can only use variation in covariates between periods that are not part of the identifying pair, and uses kernel smoothing to handle the continuous covariate case.

The convergence rate of the HK estimator is N^{1/3} in the bandwidth h_N, which is slower than the \sqrt{N} rate. This means very large samples are needed for precise inference.

Dynamic Ordered Panel Models

Why Ordered Outcomes Need Their Own Dynamic Treatment

With binary outcomes: one lag y_{i,t-1} \in \{0,1\} — one parameter \gamma.

With J ordered categories, y_{i,t-1} can enter in three ways:

  1. Single continuous score: \gamma \cdot y_{i,t-1} — assumes equal spacing, restrictive
  2. J-1 category dummies: \sum_{k=1}^{J-1} \gamma_k \mathbf{1}[y_{i,t-1}=k] — fully flexible, J-1 persistence parameters
  3. Full transition matrix: P(y_{it}=j \mid y_{i,t-1}=k) — the Markov chain estimand

The transition matrix approach is the most general — it allows asymmetric persistence: escalation may differ from de-escalation, a key substantive question in conflict, repression, and disease progression research.

The distinction between the three parameterisations of the lagged ordered outcome is important. The single continuous score restriction assumes that moving from state 1 to state 2 has the same effect as moving from state 4 to state 5 — a strong and often implausible restriction. The category dummy approach is much more flexible: each \gamma_k captures the effect of being in state k yesterday on today’s outcome (relative to the baseline state 0).

The full transition matrix P(y_{it} = j | y_{i,t-1} = k, x_{it}, \mu_i) provides the richest description. From the category dummy parameterisation: P(y_{it} = j | y_{i,t-1} = k, x_{it}, \mu_i) = F(\kappa_j - x'_{it}\beta - \gamma_k - \mu_i) - F(\kappa_{j-1} - x'_{it}\beta - \gamma_k - \mu_i)

This shows that \gamma_k simply shifts the entire distribution of y_{it} for individuals currently in state k, through the index. The asymmetric persistence hypothesis is that \gamma_k is not constant in k — persistence differs depending on the current state.

The First-Order Markovian Ordered Probit/Logit

The ordered observation-driven Markov model:

\Pr[y_{it} \leq j \mid y_{i,t-1}, x_{it}, \mu_i] = F\!\left(\kappa_j - x'_{it}\beta - \sum_{k=1}^{J-1} \gamma_k \mathbf{1}[y_{i,t-1}=k] - \mu_i\right)

Estimands of interest:

  • \gamma_k: state-specific persistence — the shift in \Pr[y_{it} \leq j] when in state k yesterday
  • \partial \Pr[y_{it}=j] / \partial x_{it}: conditional marginal effects
  • Full J \times J predicted transition matrix P_{kj}(x_{it}, \mu_i)

The J \times J predicted transition matrix evaluated at covariate means gives the most interpretable summary of dynamic persistence.

The ordered Markov model is a natural extension of both the static ordered model (add lagged category dummies) and the dynamic binary model (extend from J=2 to J>2 categories). The key parameter is \gamma_k, which captures how being in state k yesterday affects today’s cumulative probability distribution.

Note that when J = 2 (binary case), \gamma_1 = \gamma and the model reduces to the standard dynamic probit/logit. When all \gamma_k are equal to some common \gamma, the model reduces to the ordered probit/logit with a single lag treated as a continuous score.

For the initial conditions problem: y_{i0} is an ordered categorical variable taking J values. In the Wooldridge (2005) framework, y_{i0} enters the model through J-1 dummy variables (or a continuous score approximation). The group means \bar{x}_i are also included. The Mundlak-type specification then allows \mu_i to be correlated with both y_{i0} and x_i.

Application: Democracy, Repression, and Path Dependence

Walker (2006, working paper): “Democracy and Human Rights Abuses: Insights from First-Order Markov Models”

Data: 1976–2003, country-year panel; six measures of human rights abuses including the Political Terror Scale (PTS, 1–5) and CIRI physical integrity index.

Key finding: The effect of political democracy on human rights abuses depends critically on the past level of repression. The \gamma_k parameters reveal:

  • At low prior repression (k = 1, 2): democracy significantly limits future abuses
  • At high prior repression (k = 4, 5): democracy has no discernible constraining effect

Methodological contribution: A static ordered probit cannot recover this history-contingent relationship. The Markov structure is essential for the substantive finding.

Walker’s (2006) paper is a clean illustration of why ordered Markov models are needed in comparative politics and IR research. The PTS runs from 1 (secure rule of law, rare political murder) to 5 (terror extended to the whole population). Repression scores are highly persistent (autocorrelation typically above 0.8 in the raw data).

The finding that democracy helps at low repression levels but not at high ones has important policy implications: democracy promotion may be sufficient to prevent deterioration of human rights in moderately repressive states but insufficient to reduce repression in highly repressive states. This is a nuanced conclusion that requires distinguishing the effect at different points in the state space — exactly what the \gamma_k parameters capture.

Walker’s approach specifies a Bayesian or ML ordered probit where the lagged PTS category enters through J-1 = 4 dummy variables. The estimation is essentially an augmented xtoprobit with lagged category dummies and group-mean controls.

Related applications: Schnakenberg & Fariss (2014) develop a dynamic ordinal IRT model for human rights measurement that handles temporal dependence through a different mechanism (latent state space). Hill & Jones (2014) use dynamic ordered probit specifications for the PTS in their APSR study of state repression.

Dynamic Count Panel Models

INGARCH and Score-Driven Models for Counts

Davis, Dunsmuir & Streett (2003) / Harvey & Fernandes (1989): The observation-driven INGARCH(1,1):

y_{it} \mid \mathcal{F}_{i,t-1} \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_{it}), \quad \lambda_{it} = \omega_i + \alpha y_{i,t-1} + \beta \lambda_{i,t-1}

Closed-form likelihood as a product of Poisson densities. Persistence: \alpha + \beta; stationary if \alpha + \beta < 1.

Creal, Koopman & Lucas (2013) GAS framework: Update \lambda_{it} via the scaled score:

\lambda_{it} = \omega + A \cdot s_{i,t-1} + B \cdot \lambda_{i,t-1}, \quad s_{it} = \nabla_{it} \cdot \mathcal{I}_{it}^{-1}

Distribution Score s_{it} Model
Poisson y_{it}/\lambda_{it} - 1 INGARCH(1,1)
Negative Binomial (y_{it} - \lambda_{it})/(\lambda_{it}(1 + \alpha^{-1}\lambda_{it})) NB-GAS

The INGARCH(1,1) model is the count data analogue of the GARCH(1,1) model for volatility. Just as GARCH models the conditional variance as a function of past squared returns and past variances, INGARCH models the conditional mean count as a function of past counts and past conditional means. The closed-form likelihood is a key computational advantage.

Harvey and Fernandes (1989) introduced a related model using the gamma-Poisson conjugate structure. Davis, Dunsmuir and Streett (2003) provide a more general treatment with rigorous asymptotic theory.

The GAS framework provides a principled way to construct observation-driven models for any distributional family. The key insight is to use the scaled score of the predictive likelihood as the “innovation” that updates the time-varying parameter. This ensures that the update is in the direction of steepest ascent of the log-likelihood — intuitively, the parameter update responds to how surprising the current observation is relative to the previous period’s prediction.

For the Negative Binomial case: the score-based update naturally down-weights extreme counts, providing robustness to outliers. This is analogous to how the Beta-t-EGARCH model is more robust than standard GARCH for financial returns.

Dynamic FE Poisson: Blundell, Griffith & Windmeijer (2002)

The pre-sample mean approach for dynamic count panel models:

\log \lambda_{it} = x'_{it}\beta + \gamma \log y_{i,t-1} + \log \bar{y}_{i,\text{pre}} + v_{it}

where \bar{y}_{i,\text{pre}} is the pre-sample mean count — a proxy for \exp(\mu_i).

Implementation in Stata:

gen log_pre = log(pre_sample_mean + 0.5)
xtpoisson y x1 x2 l.y log_pre, fe

In R with fixest:

fepois(y ~ x1 + x2 + log(lag_y) + offset(log_pre_mean) | id, data = df)

Arellano-Bond GMM alternative (Windmeijer 2000): Instrument y_{i,t-1} with y_{i,t-2}, y_{i,t-3}, \ldots in first-differenced equations. Consistent under serial uncorrelation of v_{it}.

Blundell, Griffith and Windmeijer (2002) proposed the pre-sample mean approach as a practical way to handle individual effects in dynamic count panel models. The key assumption is that \bar{y}_{i,\text{pre}} is a sufficient statistic for \exp(\mu_i) given the in-sample regressors. This is an approximation — the true sufficient statistic for \mu_i in the static Poisson model is \sum_t y_{it}, but in the dynamic model, \sum_t y_{it} is itself correlated with \gamma y_{i,t-1}, making the conditioning argument more complex.

In the HHG (1984) patents application, Blundell et al. use 5 years of pre-sample patent counts as the proxy for \mu_i. They find that the pre-sample mean approach gives results very similar to the conditional Poisson estimator, with somewhat higher efficiency because it uses the between-firm variation that is discarded by conditioning.

Overdispersion, NB Dynamics, and Testing

NB-INGARCH: Replace Poisson with NB in the INGARCH framework:

y_{it} \mid \mathcal{F}_{i,t-1} \sim \text{NB}(\lambda_{it}, \phi), \quad \lambda_{it} = \omega + \alpha y_{i,t-1} + \beta \lambda_{i,t-1}

Available via tscount::tsglm(distr = "nbinom") in R.

Dynamic hurdle models: For excess zeros with dynamics:

  1. Participation equation: \Pr[y_{it} > 0] — dynamic binary logit
  2. Count equation: y_{it} \mid y_{it} > 0 — dynamic Poisson/NB (truncated)

Testing dynamics:

  • LR test comparing static vs dynamic Poisson
  • Check residual ACF after xtpoisson, fe for remaining serial correlation
  • Test H_0: \gamma = 0 (no lagged count effect) via Wald test

The NB-INGARCH model extends the Poisson INGARCH to handle overdispersion. The overdispersion parameter \phi is time-invariant but the conditional mean \lambda_{it} varies dynamically. The likelihood is available in closed form as a product of NB densities, making estimation straightforward.

For the hurdle model with panel data, the participation equation (binary logit/probit with FE or RE) determines whether any event occurs, and the count equation (truncated Poisson or NB with FE or RE) models the number of events conditional on participation. The dynamic extension allows past participation and past counts to affect both components. This is relevant for conflict event data where countries may be “in conflict” in some periods and not others, and the intensity of conflict (number of events) conditional on being in conflict may also exhibit dynamics.

In R, the tscount package implements INGARCH models for individual time series but does not directly handle panel data. For panel applications, one can loop over individuals and pool estimates, or use fixest with lagged outcome regressors and the QMLE Poisson.

Simulation Estimators

Why Simulation? The Multinomial Panel Probit

For a multinomial probit panel model with M alternatives and T periods, the choice probability involves a T(M-1)-dimensional integral:

\Pr[\mathbf{y}_i = \mathbf{j}] = \int_{\mathcal{A}_j} f(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_i)\, d\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_i

where \mathcal{A}_j is a high-dimensional region and \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{T(M-1)}.

  • For M = 4, T = 8: 24-dimensional integral — infeasible by quadrature
  • GHK simulator (Geweke-Hajivassiliou-Keane): factors the joint probability into a product of univariate conditionals — each integral is one-dimensional
  • MSM sidesteps the high-dimensional integral by simulating choice sequences from the model

Keane (1994) identifies three key advantages of the Method of Simulated Moments (MSM) over quadrature-based MLE for panel limited dependent variable models: 1. MSM is asymptotically as efficient as MLE in simulation size 2. MSM handles serial correlation patterns that quadrature MLE cannot 3. MSM extends to nonlinear systems of equations that are intractable with MLE

The GHK simulator factored the T(M-1)-dimensional integral into a product of univariate conditional probabilities using the Cholesky decomposition of the error covariance matrix. This makes each integral a standard normal CDF evaluation, which is fast and accurate. The GHK simulator was independently developed by Geweke (1991), Hajivassiliou and McFadden (1998), and Keane (1990), and is the most widely used simulator for discrete choice models.

The GHK Simulator

Step 1: Decompose the error covariance \Sigma = LL' (Cholesky decomposition).

Step 2: Express choice probabilities as a product of univariate conditional probabilities using the triangular structure of L.

Step 3: Draw antithetic samples \eta^{(r)} \sim \text{Normal}(0, I), r = 1, \ldots, R.

Step 4: Compute simulated probability:

\hat{P}_{ij} = \frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^R \prod_{t=1}^T \Phi\!\left(\frac{u_{it}^{(r)} - \ell'_{it}\eta^{(r)}_{<t}}{\ell_{tt}}\right)

Simulated MLE: \max_\theta \sum_i \log \hat{P}_{iy_i}(\theta) — consistent as R, N \to \infty; bias O(1/R).

Practical rule of thumb: R \geq 10\sqrt{N} draws (Hajivassiliou & Ruud 1994). For N = 1000, R \geq 316 draws — feasible on modern hardware.

The GHK simulator works by sequential conditioning. For the multivariate normal case with correlation structure \Sigma, the joint probability is factored as:

\Pr[y_1 \leq b_1, y_2 \leq b_2, \ldots] = \Pr[y_1 \leq b_1] \cdot \Pr[y_2 \leq b_2 | y_1 \leq b_1] \cdots

Each conditional probability is a univariate normal CDF, which can be computed exactly. The draws from the truncated normal conditional distributions are obtained using the inverse CDF method, and the Cholesky decomposition L propagates the correlation structure through the sequential conditioning.

Keane (1994) shows that for “moderately large” T (say T = 10) and M = 4 alternatives, R = 20–40 draws are sufficient for the simulated MLE to have negligible bias. For larger T or M, more draws are needed. The antithetic sampling (using \eta and -\eta as pairs) substantially reduces simulation variance.

MSM vs Simulated MLE: Practical Guidance

When to prefer MSM (Keane 1994):

  • MLE computationally infeasible (large T, large M)
  • Complex or unknown serial correlation patterns
  • Objective is to match moments rather than maximise likelihood

When to prefer simulated MLE:

  • Moderate T and M, large R feasible
  • Distributional assumption credible
  • Efficiency paramount (SML \sim MLE in simulation size)

Software: Stata asmprobit and asroprobit; R mlogit::mlogit() with probit = TRUE; custom GHK implementation for complex models.

The practical choice between MSM and simulated MLE often depends on computational resources and the severity of the distributional assumption. In modern practice, simulated MLE with the GHK simulator is the default for dynamic multinomial probit models, since software implementations (Stata’s asmprobit, R’s mlogit package with simulation) have made it accessible.

Zhang and Lee (2004) propose the Accelerated Importance Sampling (AIS) procedure for SML estimation of dynamic discrete choice models with long panels, which outperforms the standard GHK sampler when T is large (above 50). They illustrate with firm dividend decisions using quarterly data over 54 quarters.

For the static multinomial probit, the GHK simulator with R = 50–100 draws is typically sufficient. For dynamic models with T = 8–16, R = 100–200 draws are recommended. Performance is strongly dataset-dependent.

Censored and Truncated Panel Data Models

The Panel Tobit Model

Standard Tobit: y^*_{it} = x'_{it}\beta + \mu_i + v_{it}, v_{it} \sim \text{IIN}(0, \sigma_v^2)

Observed: y_{it} = \max(0, y^*_{it}) — hours worked, expenditure, donations.

Heckman & MaCurdy (1980): Fixed effects Tobit with life-cycle motivation. FE Tobit MLE maximises \sum_i \ln L_i(\beta, \mu_i, \sigma_v) simultaneously — suffers from the incidental parameters problem.

Random effects Tobit: Assume \mu_i \sim \text{IIN}(0, \sigma_\mu^2); Butler-Moffitt quadrature applies:

L_i = \int \prod_t \left[\Phi\!\left(\frac{x'_{it}\beta + \mu_i}{\sigma_v}\right)^{1-y_{it}}\phi\!\left(\frac{y_{it} - x'_{it}\beta - \mu_i}{\sigma_v}\right)^{y_{it}/\sigma_v}\right] \phi\!\left(\frac{\mu_i}{\sigma_\mu}\right) \frac{d\mu_i}{\sigma_\mu}

Stata: xttobit y x1 x2 (RE only; no built-in FE Tobit)

Heckman and MaCurdy (1980) motivate the FE Tobit in the context of married women’s labor supply: they argue that the life-cycle model implies correlation between \mu_i (permanent income and productivity) and x_{it} (wages, children), making the RE assumption untenable. The FE Tobit MLE simultaneously estimates \beta and \{\mu_i\}, but this suffers from the incidental parameters problem — a point Heckman and MaCurdy acknowledge and try to address by using a large T (T = 25 years of annual data from the PSID).

For the RE Tobit, the likelihood involves a single integral over \mu_i (analogous to RE probit), which is approximated by Gauss-Hermite quadrature. The Butler-Moffitt approach from applies directly.

Note that xttobit in Stata only estimates RE Tobit. For FE Tobit, the researcher must use the brute-force approach (including individual dummies, which is only feasible for small N) or use one of the semiparametric estimators discussed next.

FE Tobit MLE Bias and Honoré (1992) Trimmed LAD

Greene (2004a,b): The FE Tobit bias is approximately equal to the censoring fraction:

Censoring FE Tobit MLE Honoré LAD
< 20\% Acceptable bias May be overkill
20\%–40\% Noticeable bias Recommended
> 40\% Severe bias Essential

Honoré (1992) Trimmed LAD: Exploit symmetry of y^*_{it} around x'_{it}\beta + \mu_i to construct moment conditions free of \mu_i.

\beta_{LAD} = \argmin_\beta \sum_{i}\sum_{t>s} \left[|y_{it} - y_{is} - (x_{it}-x_{is})'\beta| - \text{trim}(\cdot)\right]

  • Consistent and asymptotically normal as N \to \infty for fixed T
  • No distributional assumption on v_{it} or \mu_i
  • Stata: ssc install honor; then honor y x1 x2, model(tobit) ll(0) group(id)

Greene (2004a,b) provides the most systematic Monte Carlo evidence on the FE Tobit MLE bias. The key findings: 1. The bias in \hat{\beta} is approximately equal to the censoring fraction — so 40% censoring gives approximately 40% bias 2. The bias is always toward zero (attenuation bias), unlike the binary choice case where bias is away from zero 3. The bias does not improve as N increases (for fixed T), confirming the theoretical result 4. Even for T = 20, the bias is non-negligible when censoring is heavy

Honoré (1992) exploits a key symmetry property: for uncensored observations, the residuals y_{it} - x'_{it}\beta - \mu_i are symmetric around zero. For censored observations, the censoring destroys this symmetry, but the trimming restores it by artificially censoring the other observation in the pair at the same point. This “symmetry restoration” generates orthogonality conditions that are free of \mu_i.

The main limitation is efficiency loss relative to the correctly-specified Tobit MLE (since it is semiparametric), and the requirement that v_{it} be IID across t (which rules out autocorrelated errors).

Dynamic Tobit and Kyriazidou Sample Selection

Honoré (1993) Dynamic Tobit: Adds y_{i,t-1} to the Tobit model:

y^*_{it} = x'_{it}\beta + \lambda y_{i,t-1} + \mu_i + v_{it}, \quad y_{it} = \max(0, y^*_{it})

Exploits symmetry conditions on v_{it} to construct GMM estimators free of \mu_i.

Kyriazidou (1997) Type 2 Tobit (sample selection model):

y^*_{1it} = x'_{1it}\beta_1 + \mu_{1i} + v_{1it} \quad \text{(selection)} y^*_{2it} = x'_{2it}\beta_2 + \mu_{2i} + v_{2it} \quad \text{(outcome, observed when } y_{1it}=1)

First-differencing eliminates \mu_{2i}; kernel weights handle selection. → Applied to nurses data.

The Kyriazidou (1997) sample selection model is the panel generalisation of the Heckman (1979) two-step estimator. The key idea is that first-differencing the outcome equation eliminates \mu_{2i}, but only for observations where the individual selects in both periods. Since the selection rule changes over time, we need to weight observations by how similar the selection index is across periods — this is the kernel weighting. The bandwidth h_N \to 0 as N \to \infty, which gives consistency at a rate slower than \sqrt{N}.

The application to the nurses’ labor supply uses this approach: participation (whether a nurse works) is modeled as the selection equation, and hours worked is the outcome equation. Both equations contain individual effects, which are eliminated by first-differencing and kernel weighting.

Selection Bias and Attrition

Panel-Specific Attrition: The Problem

Panel surveys are repeated cross-sections — individuals who drop out are permanently lost.

Two types of missing data:

  • Ignorable (MAR): Dropout is unrelated to y_{it} conditional on observables — standard panel methods remain valid
  • Nonignorable (MNAR): Dropout depends on y_{it} (even conditional on observables) — standard methods are biased

Panel-specific sources of nonignorable attrition: health deterioration, geographic mobility, survey fatigue, institution-level attrition (firm closure, country exit).

Testing ignorability: Verbeek & Nijman (1992a): include attrition-related variables (s_{it-1}, \prod_t s_{it}, T_i) as regressors — significant coefficients suggest nonignorable selection.

Verbeek and Nijman (1992a) survey the literature on nonresponse in panel surveys and propose a practical set of tests for the ignorability of the selection rule. Their key insight is that if attrition is ignorable, then the probability of being observed in the panel should not predict the value of y_{it} conditional on the regressors. By including variables related to attrition (such as the indicator for being in the sample in the previous period, the total number of waves observed, and whether the individual is in the balanced subpanel) as additional regressors in the main equation, one can test whether the selection process is related to the outcome.

These tests have the advantage of being computationally simple — they require only an OLS or GLS regression with additional regressors. The disadvantage is that they have low power if the attrition-related variables are poor proxies for the selection mechanism.

Panel Extension of Heckman’s Two-Step

Cross-section Heckman (1979): One selectivity correction term (inverse Mills ratio \lambda(\cdot)).

Panel extension (Verbeek & Nijman 1992b): Following Hausman & Wise (1979):

E[y_{it} \mid x_{it}, \mu_i, r_{it}=1] = x'_{it}\beta + \mu_i + \sigma_{\mu\varepsilon}\lambda_{\mu}(\cdot) + \sigma_{\eta v}\lambda_{\eta}(\cdot)

Two selectivity correction terms:

  1. Correlation between individual effects \mu_i and the selection disturbance
  2. Correlation between time-varying errors v_{it} and the selection disturbance

Both require numerical integration — a significant computational burden vs. the cross-section case.

Hausman and Wise (1979) showed that the panel extension of Heckman’s selectivity bias correction involves two correction terms because the panel model has two sources of disturbance: the individual effect \mu_i and the time-varying error v_{it}. The correlation between each of these and the selection disturbance generates a separate correction term.

Verbeek and Nijman (1992b) showed that the correction terms can be expressed as: - \sigma_{\mu\varepsilon}\lambda_{\mu}(\cdot): correction for correlation between \mu_i and the selection process - \sigma_{\eta v}\lambda_{\eta}(\cdot): correction for correlation between v_{it} and the selection process

Both correction terms involve the inverse Mills ratio evaluated at individual- and time-specific arguments that depend on the selection equation. Computing these requires numerical integration over the joint distribution of (\mu_i, v_{it}), which is a one-dimensional integral for the one-way error component model.

In practice, many researchers use the much simpler Wooldridge (1995) variable addition tests as a first diagnostic, and only proceed to the full Verbeek-Nijman correction if the simpler tests suggest nonignorable selection.

Wooldridge (1995) Variable Addition Test and Hausman Tests

Wooldridge (1995) variable addition test:

  1. For each t, run Tobit (or probit) for the selection rule; compute residuals \hat{\varepsilon}_{it}
  2. Demean: \tilde{\varepsilon}_{it} = \hat{\varepsilon}_{it} - \bar{\hat{\varepsilon}}_{i}
  3. Run FE regression of y_{it} on x_{it} and \tilde{\varepsilon}_{it}
  4. Test H_0: \gamma = 0 — rejection suggests nonignorable selection

Verbeek & Nijman (1992a) four-estimator Hausman tests:

Let \hat{\delta} = (\tilde{\beta}_W(B),\; \tilde{\beta}_W(U),\; \tilde{\beta}_{GLS}(B),\; \tilde{\beta}_{GLS}(U)) where B = balanced subpanel, U = full panel.

Test contrasts (R_1–R_4) compare Within vs GLS estimators across balanced and unbalanced subpanels — all should agree under ignorable selection.

Wooldridge (1995) proposes computationally simple variable addition tests for selection bias that avoid the need for numerical integration. The tests use either Tobit residuals or inverse Mills ratios as additional regressors in the FE equation, and test whether these variables are significant. If they are, it suggests that the selection process is related to the outcome beyond what the observables can explain.

The test is a t-test on the coefficient of the added variable in the FE regression. Wooldridge (1995) shows that under the null hypothesis of ignorable selection, the test statistic is asymptotically N(0,1) if E[v_{it}v'_{it}/\mu_i, x'_i, s_i] = \sigma^2 I_T (no serial correlation or heteroskedasticity in the idiosyncratic errors). If this assumption is violated, cluster-robust standard errors should be used.

The advantage of this approach over the Verbeek-Nijman correction is that it is much easier to implement: it requires only standard FE regression with one additional regressor. The disadvantage is that it does not provide consistent estimates of the selection-corrected \beta if selection bias is present — it only tests for its presence.

Verbeek and Nijman (1992a) propose the four-estimator framework for testing ignorable selection in unbalanced panels. The key insight is that if selection is ignorable, all four estimators should give similar results (up to sampling variance). Systematic differences suggest that the selection process is related to the outcome.

Empirical Application — Nurses’ Labour Supply

Data and Research Design

Askildsen, Baltagi & Holmås (2003): Do wages raise female labour supply among nurses?

Data: 19,638 Norwegian female nurses, 1993–98; six-year matched panel; 69,122 observations.

Three simultaneous challenges:

  • Individual heterogeneity: Nurses differ in unobserved productivity, preferences, family responsibilities
  • Sample selection: Hours observed only for participating nurses
  • Endogeneity: High-productivity nurses earn more AND work more

Estimation strategy (Kyriazidou 1997):

  1. Step 1: Conditional logit (FE) participation equation; kernel weights constructed
  2. Step 2: Kyriazidou IV hours equation using lagged municipality financial surplus and auxiliary nurse wage as instruments

The Askildsen, Baltagi and Holmås (2003) study is an exemplary application of the methods in this chapter: it combines fixed effects binary choice (participation), sample selection correction (Kyriazidou 1997), and instrumental variables in a single coherent framework. The panel structure with six years of data is long enough to apply conditional logit and the Kyriazidou estimator.

The key exclusion restrictions are: (i) the lagged net financial surplus of the municipality (which affects wages through the budget constraint but not directly the nurse’s hours conditional on participation), and (ii) the average wage of auxiliary nurses in the same municipality (which provides wage variation not driven by the individual nurse’s productivity). These instruments pass the Hausman overidentification test.

The study is reproduced in Baltagi (2008) as the empirical illustration for Chapter 11.

The vce(cluster id) option is essential in all panel GLM estimations — it accounts for within-individual correlation of errors across time periods that is not fully modeled by the individual effects. Standard errors without clustering understate the true uncertainty.

Workflow, Diagnostics, and Common Pitfalls

Step 1 — Test for individual effects:

xttest0     // LM test H0: sigma_mu = 0
hausman fe re, sigmamore  // FE vs RE Hausman

Step 2 — Test for serial correlation:

xtserial y x1 x2  // Wooldridge test (install via SSC)

Step 3 — Test for selection (if unbalanced):

* Wooldridge (1995) variable addition test
* or compare Within and GLS on balanced vs unbalanced

Common pitfalls:

  • Not clustering SEs at the individual level
  • Using xtnbreg, fe without understanding the Allison-Waterman caveat
  • Ignoring non-switchers in FE logit (check: how many?)
  • Running xttobit when censoring fraction is high (> 30\%)
  • Reporting raw \hat{\beta} coefficients without average partial effects

The workflow described here is a practical guide for applied researchers. The key diagnostic tests are:

  1. xttest0 (after xtreg or xtprobit/xtpoisson, re): Tests H_0: \sigma_\mu^2 = 0. If not rejected, pooled estimation is consistent.
  2. Hausman test: Tests H_0: E[\mu_i | x_{it}] = 0. If rejected, FE (or correlated RE) is needed.
  3. xtserial (Stata, install via SSC): Wooldridge (2010) test for serial correlation in the idiosyncratic errors. If rejected, the standard RE/FE assumptions about v_{it} may be violated.
  4. Variable addition test (Wooldridge 1995): Tests for nonignorable selection bias in unbalanced panels.

The “non-switchers” issue with xtlogit, fe: Stata will print “obs dropped due to no variation” — if this is a large fraction of the sample, the FE logit estimates may be unrepresentative.

For cluster-robust standard errors in R: the clubSandwich package provides vcovCR(model, cluster = df$id, type = "CR1S") for cluster-robust variance estimation compatible with most model classes.

Synthesis and Open Frontiers

Decision Flowchart

Step 1 — What type of outcome?

Binary → Blocks 2/3/6A | Ordered → Blocks 4/6B | Count → Blocks 5/6C | Censored → Block 8 | Selection → Block 9

Step 2 — FE or RE?

Run Mundlak test (test xbar1 xbar2 ...). Reject H_0 → Use FE (or correlated RE) | Fail to reject → RE sufficient

Step 3 — Is there temporal dependence?

Run serial correlation test (xtserial / pbgtest). Reject H_0 → Add lagged outcome; address initial conditions (Wooldridge 2005 device)

Step 4 — Is the panel balanced?

Unbalanced → Test ignorability of attrition (Wooldridge 1995 variable addition test). Reject → Full selection correction (Block 9)

Step 5 — Report average partial effects, not raw coefficients. Use margins / marginaleffects.

This flowchart provides a practical decision guide for researchers. The key principle is to start with the simplest defensible specification and add complexity only when diagnostics demand it.

A common mistake is to jump directly to complex estimation procedures (dynamic FE ordered logit, Kyriazidou sample selection) without first verifying that the simpler models fail. If tests for individual effects, serial correlation, and selection bias all pass, the pooled estimator with cluster-robust standard errors may be sufficient.

The instruction to report average partial effects (APEs) rather than coefficients is especially important for nonlinear models: the coefficient \hat{\beta} in a probit or Poisson model does not have a direct causal or policy interpretation without transformation. The APE, averaged over the empirical distribution of covariates, provides the natural analogue of the OLS coefficient in a linear model.

Open Frontiers and Current Developments

Partial identification in FE models: Honoré & Tamer (2006) provide informative bounds on \beta when point identification fails in FE binary choice. Extension to ordered and dynamic cases (Müller et al. 2021) is active research.

Machine learning and high-dimensional fixed effects: fixest handles millions of observations and thousands of fixed effects. Current research explores regularisation (LASSO, ridge) for selecting controls while maintaining consistent \hat{\beta} in panel GLMs.

Bayesian panel GLMs: Hasegawa (2009) — Bayesian dynamic ordered probit via MCMC. Schnakenberg & Fariss (2014) — dynamic ordinal IRT. Particularly valuable for short panels (T \leq 4) where frequentist FE methods discard too much information.

Non-rectangular panels and multiple imputation: Multiple imputation for missing panel data (Rubin 1987 extended to panel settings); pattern mixture models for MNAR data.

These open frontiers point to active research directions that students at the advanced PhD level may wish to pursue.

The partial identification approach (Honoré & Tamer 2006) is particularly promising for social science applications where identifying assumptions for point identification are implausible. Partial identification provides honest bounds on parameters rather than forcing point estimates that rest on questionable assumptions.

Machine learning methods for panel GLMs are developing rapidly. The fixest package already implements some of these, and the DoubleML package in R provides double/debiased ML estimators for panel data models with high-dimensional covariates.

For Bayesian methods: Hasegawa (2009) implements a Bayesian dynamic ordered probit model via MCMC that handles the initial conditions problem naturally through the prior on \mu_i | y_{i0}. This approach is particularly appealing for very short panels where the Wooldridge device may be imprecise.
